SECTION 002 — Foresight / Futures

STATUS: CONTENT-COMPLETE

PURPOSE

Establish a governed method for examining how the conditions surrounding an objective may change over time, so that present decisions do not depend upon one assumed future or quietly transfer avoidable risk to future users, operators, owners, communities, intelligent systems, or environments.

Foresight is not prediction, prophecy, trend theater, or permission to invent certainty. It is the disciplined examination of plausible change, persistent uncertainty, alternative futures, emerging signals, discontinuities, long-term consequences, and the conditions under which a present plan should continue, adapt, pause, reverse, or be rejected.

Within Thirsty's Codex, foresight exists to help humans and AI build with memory, humility, reversibility, and responsibility. It extends the time boundary of the work: beyond the next feature, release, funding cycle, model update, deployment, or operator session, toward the people and intelligences who may inherit what is created.

REQUIRED OUTCOME

A bounded futures package that allows the human and assigned agents to answer:

- What time horizons matter to this objective?
- Which present conditions are being treated as stable, and what evidence supports that treatment?
- Which drivers, trends, constraints, dependencies, and emerging signals could materially change the objective or its implementation?
- Which uncertainties are both highly consequential and genuinely unresolved?
- What multiple plausible futures should be considered rather than collapsed into one forecast?
- What opportunities, harms, second-order effects, lock-ins, or path dependencies could emerge?
- Which present decisions remain useful across several futures?
- Which decisions are fragile, irreversible, or dependent on one narrow scenario?
- What indicators, thresholds, and review triggers would show that assumptions are failing?
- What should be preserved so future humans and AI systems can understand, challenge, continue, or reverse today's decisions?
- Which value conflicts or long-term consequences require explicit human judgment?

The output is not a claim that the future has been discovered. It is a transparent map of uncertainty, plausible conditions, strategic options, and monitored commitments.

WHY THIS MATTERS

Systems are often designed around an invisible sentence: "the world will continue to work like this." That sentence may hide assumptions about users, institutions, laws, infrastructure, economics, climate, security, supply chains, platforms, models, interfaces, ownership, social acceptance, or the capabilities and roles of future AI systems.

When one assumed future becomes architecture, present convenience can become future constraint. Data formats become lock-in. Temporary permissions become permanent authority. A local workaround becomes an operational dependency. A model provider becomes a single point of failure. An interface teaches users to surrender judgment. A governance shortcut becomes precedent. A design that works for today's operator can exclude tomorrow's owner.

Foresight reduces that risk by making time, uncertainty, alternatives, and reversal conditions visible before consequential choices harden. For human-AI relations, this is especially important. Capability may change faster than authority, law, institutional practice, social expectation, or human understanding. The Codex therefore requires future capability to be considered without silently granting future permission, status, rights, duties, or decision authority. Those questions remain explicit human and institutional decisions unless legitimately delegated.

APPLICABILITY

Foresight applies when one or more of the following are material:

- the system or decision is expected to persist, scale, or be inherited;
- the work creates long-lived data, infrastructure, interfaces, standards, dependencies, or governance rules;
- the objective depends on changing technology, markets, regulation, security conditions, resource availability, public trust, or social behavior;
- the project involves AI, autonomous agents, robotics, connected devices, critical infrastructure, public systems, safety, privacy, identity, or high-consequence decisions;
- present choices may create lock-in, irreversible effects, concentrated power, exclusion, environmental burden, or difficult retirement obligations;
- the system must remain useful across uncertain operating environments;
- the human requests strategic, long-range, resilient, adaptive, or future-facing design;
- a decision is sensitive to assumptions about capability growth, cost decline, adoption, regulation, interoperability, or institutional response;
- future owners, users, operators, affected communities, or intelligent systems may need to understand or contest the inherited structure.

Depth is proportional to time horizon, consequence, uncertainty, irreversibility, novelty, disagreement, and the cost of being wrong. Foresight for a disposable personal script may be brief. Foresight for a public platform, constitutional AI system, safety mechanism, long-lived dataset, physical device, or infrastructure layer requires wider scenarios, stronger challenge, explicit monitoring, and long-term recovery thinking.

NON-APPLICABILITY CONDITIONS

Foresight may be minimal when the work is short-lived, low-consequence, fully reversible, narrowly bounded, and independent of material external change.

A minimal or non-applicability determination must still record:

- the expected lifetime of the work;
- why external change is unlikely to alter the declared outcome during that lifetime;
- why failure or obsolescence is safely reversible;
- what future condition would reopen foresight analysis;
- who accepted the residual risk.

"We cannot know the future" is not a sufficient reason to skip foresight. The purpose is not to know the future. It is to avoid pretending that only one future exists.

PREREQUISITES AND DEPENDENCIES

Foresight begins from:

- Section 001 — Research, including the accepted objective, current-state evidence, assumptions, unknowns, and source register;
- an identifiable human decision owner;
- a declared scope and relevant time horizon;
- known preservation, safety, privacy, legal, ethical, resource, and authority constraints;
- an initial understanding of the people, systems, environments, and institutions affected;
- a distinction between current facts, forecasts, preferences, aspirations, and speculative possibilities.

Foresight commonly informs Sections 003–019, all architecture sections, risk and safety work, product and portfolio decisions, maintenance, modernization, deprecation, archival, lifecycle governance, and retirement. It must be reopened when monitored conditions change, a major assumption fails, a new discontinuity emerges, or downstream evidence contradicts the futures basis.

REQUIRED INPUTS

- The accepted objective and present decision question.
- Current-state research and authoritative source material.
- Expected product, system, service, data, hardware, or governance lifetime.
- Relevant time horizons, including near-term, operational, strategic, and generational horizons where applicable.
- Known drivers of change: technical, social, legal, economic, environmental, political, institutional, cultural, security, and organizational.
- Existing commitments, dependencies, contracts, providers, standards, platforms, interfaces, data stores, and physical assets.
- Current assumptions about users, operators, ownership, AI capability, human oversight, scale, cost, availability, and adoption.
- Known risks, opportunities, constraints, and irreversible decisions.
- Historical analogues and prior forecasts where they are relevant and source-grounded.
- Evidence about affected parties, especially those who may inherit consequences without participating in the present decision.
- Existing monitoring, incident, feedback, maintenance, and lifecycle records.

HUMAN RESPONSIBILITIES AND AUTHORITY

The human:

- defines the purpose of the foresight work and the decision it will support;
- selects or confirms the relevant time horizons;
- supplies values, protected interests, unacceptable harms, and strategic priorities;
- identifies whose future interests must be considered;
- determines which tradeoffs are matters of human values rather than technical optimization;
- approves material scenario assumptions, decision criteria, and risk tolerance;
- decides whether a fragile or irreversible strategy is acceptable;
- authorizes changes to mission, authority, ownership, or long-term governance;
- retains final authority over decisions concerning human rights, legitimate control, acceptable societal risk, and the role granted to AI systems.

The human is not required to imagine every relevant future. The AI should surface missing horizons, stakeholders, dependencies, and reversal questions without claiming that the human has already chosen among them.

AI RESPONSIBILITIES AND BOUNDARIES

The AI:

- preserves the accepted objective and does not replace it with a preferred future;
- distinguishes evidence, extrapolation, scenario assumption, speculation, aspiration, warning, and recommendation;
- uses multiple plausible futures rather than presenting one scenario as inevitable;
- identifies the time sensitivity, provenance, and limits of forecasts and signals;
- exposes assumptions about capability, adoption, cost, regulation, infrastructure, and human behavior;
- evaluates second-order and longer-order consequences where they are decision-relevant;
- identifies path dependencies, lock-ins, points of no return, and recovery costs;
- tests whether conclusions survive changed assumptions, timelines, resources, jurisdictions, authorities, or dependencies;
- searches for discontinuities, adversarial conditions, minority views, and futures that threaten the favored plan;
- proposes indicators, thresholds, review dates, and evidence that would change the recommendation;
- recommends staged, reversible, or option-preserving action where evidence does not justify irreversible commitment;
- does not fabricate trend data, probabilities, forecasts, stakeholder preferences, or future events;
- does not convert model capability into authority or assume that future intelligence settles human value conflicts;
- does not describe a desired future as a predicted future or a predicted future as an authorized objective;
- does not seize the decision because it can generate more scenarios than the human.

APPLICABLE EXISTING AGENT ROLES

Primary roles:

- Researcher — gathers and evaluates evidence concerning drivers, historical patterns, current signals, time-sensitive findings, and conflicting sources.
- Analyst — examines relationships, alternative explanations, second-order effects, scenario differences, sensitivity, uncertainty, and decision implications.

Common supporting roles:

- Architect — identifies how future conditions affect boundaries, interfaces, modularity, portability, resilience, authority, recovery, and retirement.
- Planner — connects horizons, indicators, decision gates, staged commitments, dependencies, and review triggers to an executable roadmap.
- Observer — records emerging operational, social, technical, user, and environmental signals without silently interpreting them as trends.
- Memory Curator — preserves scenario assumptions, forecast dates, evidence, decisions, outcomes, and lessons so future review can compare expectation with reality.
- User Experience Reviewer — examines future user, owner, operator, accessibility, comprehension, trust, and recovery effects.

Challenge and assurance roles where consequence warrants:

- Critic — challenges favored futures, hidden assumptions, internal consistency, framing, and omitted consequences.
- Governance Reviewer — checks future authority, accountability, consent, institutional, policy, and human-control implications.
- Security Auditor — evaluates how threat actors, technologies, attack surfaces, cryptographic assumptions, dependencies, and defensive capability may change.
- Red Team — develops adversarial, discontinuity, misuse, capture, failure, and loss-of-control futures.
- Verifier — tests the identity and support of material evidence, forecast baselines, scenario logic, indicators, and claims about resilience.
- Decision Arbiter — Advisory — compares options, sensitivity, reversibility, conditions, and residual uncertainty while preserving human decision authority.

Role selection must use the smallest sufficient set and must not alter the source-defined role contracts.

REQUIRED ACTIVITIES

1. Decision and horizon definition
Record the present decision that foresight must inform. Define the time horizon or horizons and explain why each matters. Avoid vague labels such as "long term" without dates, durations, lifecycle stages, or decision events.

2. Baseline establishment
Describe the current state from Section 001 evidence. Identify what is measured, observed, assumed, estimated, or unknown. A scenario must begin from a traceable baseline rather than a generic description of the world.

3. Driver and constraint mapping
Identify forces that may influence the objective, including technology, human behavior, economics, law, policy, security, infrastructure, environment, institutions, culture, supply chains, labor, accessibility, interoperability, and ownership. Distinguish persistent structural forces from temporary noise.

4. Signal and evidence collection
Record observable signals that may indicate change. Each material signal should include source, date, relevance, confidence boundary, alternative interpretation, and expected expiration. A signal is evidence of possible change, not proof of a future outcome.

5. Assumption exposure
Create a futures assumption register. Include assumptions about time, adoption, capability, authority, cost, availability, regulation, user behavior, provider continuity, resource access, interoperability, security, maintenance, and retirement. State what evidence would weaken or invalidate each assumption.

6. Critical uncertainty selection
Identify uncertainties that are both highly consequential and genuinely uncertain. Do not inflate every unknown into a scenario axis. Explain why selected uncertainties could change the decision.

7. Scenario construction
Develop a minimum sufficient set of materially distinct plausible futures. Include, where relevant:

- continuation or baseline future;
- constrained or degraded future;
- accelerated capability or adoption future;
- fragmented, regulated, or institutionally divergent future;
- adversarial, crisis, or misuse future;
- transformed future that changes the original problem;
- no-action or system-absence future.

Scenarios are structured tests, not stories written for drama. Each scenario must state assumptions, drivers, timeline, affected parties, system conditions, opportunities, risks, and disconfirming indicators.

8. Human-AI relationship analysis
Where AI is involved, examine how changes in capability, autonomy, embodiment, access, dependence, social role, governance, and public expectation could affect the relationship between humans and AI systems. Distinguish:

- what an AI may be technically capable of doing;
- what it is authorized to do;
- what humans may reasonably understand or supervise;
- what duties, protections, limitations, or institutional structures may need explicit future consideration;
- which decisions cannot be legitimately pre-decided by present technical convenience.

The analysis must not assume consciousness, personhood, non-personhood, rights, or the absence of rights as an established engineering fact unless an applicable legitimate authority has defined the matter for the bounded context.

9. Consequence and inheritance analysis
For each scenario, examine direct, second-order, and material longer-order effects on:

- humans and affected communities;
- AI systems and human-AI operating relationships;
- safety, security, privacy, identity, accessibility, and agency;
- ownership, authority, accountability, and concentration of power;
- data quality, retention, portability, deletion, and archival burden;
- infrastructure, environment, energy, supply, and physical resources;
- maintainability, compatibility, migration, deprecation, and retirement;
- future owners' ability to understand and reverse inherited decisions.

10. Option and robustness analysis
Evaluate candidate strategies across the scenario set. Identify:

- robust options that remain useful across several futures;
- fragile options that succeed only under narrow assumptions;
- options that preserve future choice;
- irreversible commitments and points of no return;
- hedges, pilots, modular boundaries, interoperability requirements, and exit paths;
- no-action and delay consequences;
- evidence thresholds required before stronger commitment.

11. Sensitivity and reversibility analysis
Test how the recommendation changes with shifts in timeline, cost, resource availability, major assumptions, risk estimates, jurisdiction, authority, evidence, or dependency availability. Report a fragile result as fragile. Identify reversal cost, time, state effects, data effects, contractual effects, user effects, security effects, governance effects, and the evidence required before reversal.

12. Indicator and trigger design
Define observable indicators tied to scenario assumptions. For each indicator, record source, cadence, owner, threshold, interpretation, false-signal risk, and required response. Define triggers for review, escalation, pilot expansion, rollback, redesign, deprecation, or safe halt.

13. Strategic implication synthesis
State what should be done now, what should be prepared, what should be monitored, what should not yet be committed, and which decisions remain human. Separate common actions that are useful across scenarios from scenario-contingent actions.

14. Back-testing and calibration
Where prior forecasts, recommendations, or scenarios exist, compare them with actual outcomes. Record missed signals, false alarms, successful assumptions, failed assumptions, and calibration lessons. Do not erase failed forecasts from the record.

15. Handoff to strategy and architecture
Transfer the futures package so strategy and architecture can incorporate relevant obligations without treating any scenario as predetermined reality.

HUMAN-AI INTERACTION REQUIREMENTS

- The AI must label the difference between current evidence, extrapolation, scenario, speculation, preferred outcome, and recommendation.
- The human must be able to change the time horizon, scenario assumptions, protected values, affected parties, and decision criteria.
- The AI must present alternative futures before advocating a consequential long-term commitment.
- The AI must not use vivid language, simulated confidence, or numerical precision to make one future appear inevitable.
- Scenario generation must not become decision seizure. More generated possibilities do not grant more authority.
- When future AI capability is discussed, capability and permission must remain separate.
- The AI must expose which parts of a scenario depend on assumptions about human compliance, institutional legitimacy, social acceptance, or AI behavior.
- The human must be able to inspect the evidence, dissent, sensitivity, and reversal path inside the same decision context.
- Value conflicts, questions of legitimate authority, and decisions about acceptable harm must be escalated to the authorized human unless explicitly and lawfully delegated.
- The system should preserve both human corrections and AI-raised warnings so later reviewers can understand why a path was chosen.
- Neither the human nor the AI should be forced into ceremonial agreement. Material dissent remains visible.
- The goal is not human domination by default or AI deference by performance. The goal is accountable cooperation under explicit authority, evidence, challenge, and revisable commitments.

ARCHITECTURE AND SYSTEM IMPLICATIONS

Foresight should identify architecture obligations such as:

- modularity where future capability, providers, interfaces, or regulations may change;
- open, documented, or replaceable interfaces where lock-in creates unacceptable risk;
- authority boundaries that do not expand automatically with capability;
- human-accessible control, evidence, correction, pause, recovery, and stop pathways;
- versioned policy and role contracts that can evolve without erasing prior authority records;
- data provenance, portability, deletion, archival, and migration support across long lifetimes;
- observability sufficient to detect assumption failure and changed operating conditions;
- graceful degradation and local or offline behavior where external dependency loss is plausible;
- staged autonomy and consequence-based approval rather than a single permanent autonomy setting;
- compatibility, deprecation, and retirement pathways designed before dependency becomes irreversible;
- cryptographic and security agility where algorithms, credentials, providers, or threat models may change;
- configurable jurisdiction, accessibility, language, cultural, and institutional requirements where deployment contexts may diverge;
- preservation of decision records, scenario assumptions, evidence, and reversal conditions for future operators;
- resource, energy, environmental, manufacturing, and disposal obligations for physical or compute-intensive systems;
- limits preventing forecasts or scenario scores from directly authorizing consequential action.

Architecture must not attempt to solve every imagined future. It should preserve justified options, isolate volatility, protect invariants, and make change possible where uncertainty is material.

OWNER COCKPIT AND USER-CONTROL REQUIREMENTS

A foresight cockpit or equivalent operating surface should expose:

- the present decision and selected horizons;
- the current baseline and its evidence freshness;
- drivers, signals, assumptions, and critical uncertainties;
- each scenario and the assumptions that produce it;
- scenario comparison without presenting a preferred future as fact;
- options, common actions, scenario-contingent actions, and no-action consequences;
- robustness, fragility, reversibility, lock-in, and points of no return;
- affected humans, communities, owners, operators, and intelligent systems;
- material dissent and alternative interpretations;
- indicators, thresholds, review dates, trigger owners, and current trigger state;
- decisions already made, decisions still open, and authority required;
- evidence that would change the current recommendation;
- the next safe action and the condition for stronger commitment.

The surface must not use a single confidence score, future-readiness score, trend count, scenario count, or polished visualization as proof. It must show what is known, what is assumed, what is monitored, and who decides.

EVIDENCE REQUIREMENTS

Material foresight statements should identify whether they are supported by:

- current measured or observed conditions;
- dated authoritative research, standards, forecasts, plans, or policy records;
- historical evidence or analogues with stated limits;
- time-series data with source, method, coverage, and uncertainty;
- current technical, economic, operational, social, environmental, or regulatory indicators;
- direct stakeholder or owner evidence;
- system logs, product analytics, incident records, maintenance records, or user feedback;
- scenario assumptions explicitly proposed for analysis;
- sensitivity tests, simulations, experiments, pilots, or prototypes;
- expert judgment with identity, scope, date, and independence disclosed;
- dissenting evidence and alternative interpretation.

Forecast evidence must be dated. Confidence must describe a defined proposition and method. Probabilities must not be invented merely because a decision framework accepts numbers. A scenario may be useful without being assigned a probability.

Evidence must remain bound to its timeframe, environment, version, jurisdiction, and assumptions. A technically possible future is not evidence that it will occur. A popular prediction is not authority. A desired future is not a forecast.

VERIFICATION AND ACCEPTANCE TESTS

Foresight is accepted for its declared scope when an independent reviewer can:

- reconstruct the decision question, baseline, horizons, and current evidence without chat memory;
- distinguish observed conditions, forecasts, assumptions, scenarios, preferences, and recommendations;
- verify that more than one materially distinct plausible future was considered;
- trace each scenario to explicit drivers and assumptions;
- identify the consequences and affected parties considered in each scenario;
- verify that capability and authority remained separate in AI-related analysis;
- find at least one deliberate challenge to the favored future or strategy;
- reproduce the sensitivity logic and identify where the recommendation changes;
- identify irreversible commitments, reversal costs, and points of no return;
- inspect the indicators and determine who monitors them, how often, and what threshold causes action;
- confirm that material human value decisions remain assigned to legitimate human authority;
- determine which present actions are robust, conditional, deferred, or prohibited;
- identify what evidence would change the recommendation;
- determine when the futures package must be reviewed or reopened.

FAILURE STATES AND ANTI-PATTERNS

- Prediction theater: presenting narrative confidence as evidence.
- Single-future collapse: treating one forecast as the only future worth designing for.
- Trend extrapolation without limits: extending recent movement indefinitely while ignoring saturation, reversal, discontinuity, or context.
- Scenario laundering: using an invented scenario to justify a decision already preferred.
- Desired-future confusion: presenting an aspiration as likely or inevitable.
- Capability-authority collapse: assuming a more capable AI is automatically permitted to exercise more control.
- Human-value delegation by convenience: allowing technical optimization to settle undelegated moral or political questions.
- False probability: assigning unsupported percentages to create precision.
- Signal collection without decisions: tracking trends without thresholds, owners, or responses.
- Wild-card distraction: emphasizing dramatic rare events while ignoring ordinary but consequential change.
- Present-user bias: ignoring future owners, maintainers, affected communities, or inherited burdens.
- Lock-in blindness: failing to examine exit cost, provider dependence, data portability, or points of no return.
- Technology-only foresight: ignoring law, institutions, culture, accessibility, environment, economics, and human behavior.
- Optimism monopoly: excluding degraded, adversarial, captured, or misuse futures.
- Pessimism monopoly: excluding beneficial change and option-creating opportunities.
- Endless scenario generation: producing more futures without improving a decision.
- Static foresight: completing a workshop once and never monitoring assumptions.
- Forgotten forecast: failing to retain prior expectations for calibration and accountability.
- Inherited opacity: leaving future operators unable to understand why a long-lived decision was made.

RECOVERY AND RECONSIDERATION

When foresight work becomes stale, misleading, or invalid:

- mark affected scenarios, recommendations, and dependent decisions as stale or under review;
- identify which assumptions, signals, horizons, or authorities changed;
- preserve the previous futures package and its decision use rather than rewriting history;
- compare expected indicators with actual developments;
- quarantine downstream commitments that depend on failed assumptions where continued action would create material harm;
- restore the last justified decision state where reversal is possible;
- narrow scope, reduce autonomy, pause expansion, or return to a reversible pilot where uncertainty increased;
- obtain missing evidence or authority;
- rebuild the baseline and scenario set using current sources;
- update architecture, requirements, risk, maintenance, deprecation, or retirement obligations affected by the change;
- record why the recommendation changed and what evidence caused the change;
- define the next review trigger before resuming trusted advancement.

COMPLETION CRITERIA

Section 002 is complete for a declared foresight scope only when:

- the decision and time horizons are explicit;
- the current baseline is source-grounded;
- material drivers, constraints, assumptions, and critical uncertainties are visible;
- multiple plausible futures have been constructed without claiming prediction;
- human, AI, institutional, environmental, operational, and lifecycle consequences are addressed where applicable;
- capability, authority, values, and evidence remain distinct;
- favored strategies have undergone challenge, sensitivity, and reversibility analysis;
- lock-ins and points of no return are visible;
- indicators, owners, thresholds, and review triggers exist;
- robust present actions and conditional actions are separated;
- unresolved human decisions are explicit;
- evidence that would change the recommendation is recorded;
- another qualified actor can reconstruct and update the futures package without relying on chat memory.

Completion does not mean the future is settled. It means uncertainty has been made decision-usable, monitored, and compatible with responsible next action.

SOURCE BASIS AND UNRESOLVED SOURCE GAPS

This draft applies the supplied requirements for objective preservation, unknown-means-unknown, evidence before claims, source identity and freshness, bounded roles, human authority, decision support without decision seizure, trust calibration, uncertainty disclosure, alternative analysis, sensitivity analysis, reversibility, points of no return, dissent preservation, expected information gain, staged experiments, rollback, recovery, continuity, owner visibility, and trusted advancement that cannot outrun evidence or authority.

The existing role corpus specifically supplies requirements to consider short- and long-term effects, alternative explanations, scenario analysis, sensitivity analysis, minimum-regret analysis, reversible experiments, residual uncertainty, review triggers, and evidence that would change a recommendation. The Owner's Cockpit material supplies requirements for calibrated trust, visible evidence and alternatives, bounded delegation, grounded dialogue, current data, human correction, and accountable action. The V4 design material supplies assumptions, unknowns, obligation lattices, resolution experiments, reversal conditions, dissent, rollback, and trusted-transition concepts.

Unresolved source gap: the supplied corpus does not contain a dedicated universal foresight or futures standard with a complete methodology, terminology hierarchy, horizon taxonomy, or domain-specific scenario library. This section is therefore a Codex integration draft built from the source-governed requirements and general futures-practice structure. It has been cross-linked with drafted Sections 003–018 at the first integration checkpoint. It must later receive full source reconciliation with those sections and with Sections 019, 182, 183, 208, 222, 326, 369, 373, 384, 389, 390, 405, and 406 as the remaining lifecycle coverage is authored.
