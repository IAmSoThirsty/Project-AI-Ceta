SECTION 024 — Domain Architecture

STATUS: CONTENT-COMPLETE

PURPOSE

Transform the source-grounded domain understanding established in Domain Analysis into a governed architecture of domain boundaries, responsibilities, language, rules, state, decisions, data ownership, services, events, authority, integrations, and lifecycle change.

Domain Architecture defines how domain meaning is partitioned and protected across the system and organization. It identifies bounded contexts, subdomains, model ownership, sources of truth, translations, contracts, invariants, decision rights, and integration boundaries so software, data, APIs, events, workflows, policies, AI systems, and human operations do not silently assign different meanings to the same concept.

This section does not declare a software class, database schema, organizational chart, or model-generated ontology to be the domain itself. It converts validated domain evidence into a controlled architectural basis while keeping disputed meaning, jurisdiction, effective date, authority, and unresolved gaps visible.

REQUIRED OUTCOME

- A declared domain-architecture scope identifying the domain, enterprise and system boundaries, decision owner, authoritative experts and sources, intended consumers, jurisdictions, lifecycle horizon, and exclusions.
- A domain landscape separating core, supporting, generic, external, regulatory, and enabling subdomains as applicable without assigning value labels unsupported by evidence.
- A bounded-context map showing each context's purpose, language, model owner, authority, source of truth, rules, state, services, events, data, evidence, interfaces, dependencies, and lifecycle.
- A context relationship map covering upstream and downstream responsibilities, published language, customer-supplier relationships, shared kernels or shared models, conformist dependencies, translation, anti-corruption layers, open-host services, and explicit isolation where applicable.
- Canonical terminology within each bounded context and controlled translation where the same word has different meanings across contexts.
- Domain models covering entities, value objects, aggregates or consistency boundaries where applicable, identifiers, classifications, policies, services, commands or intents, events, states, transitions, invariants, exceptions, time, and termination.
- Explicit distinction between domain rule, organizational policy, legal obligation, workflow convention, software behavior, data representation, model inference, recommendation, and human decision.
- Source-of-truth and authority ownership for every material domain fact, decision, state, rule, exception, and evidence object.
- Versioned domain contracts for APIs, events, data exchange, decisions, evidence, and human or AI handoffs.
- A change and evolution model covering effective dates, versioning, compatibility, migration, coexistence, deprecation, dispute, supersession, and retirement.
- A domain-to-requirement, domain-to-system, domain-to-data, domain-to-interface, domain-to-policy, and domain-to-test traceability model.
- A durable handoff to Business Architecture, Capability Architecture, Process Architecture, Functional Architecture, Operational Architecture, Information Architecture, Data Architecture, Application Architecture, Software Architecture, API Architecture, Event-Driven Architecture, AI Architecture, testing, operations, and lifecycle work.

WHY THIS MATTERS

Domain inconsistency is one of the most expensive forms of architectural debt because it spreads silently. A term can carry one meaning in a user journey, another in an API, another in a database, another in finance, and another in a model prompt. One service may treat an event as a fact while another treats it as a request. A workflow may encode an informal workaround as policy. A data record may be treated as the real-world entity. An AI classification may be treated as a decision even though no authority was delegated.

Domain Architecture confines meaning to explicit boundaries and defines how meaning crosses those boundaries. It allows different contexts to use different models where their responsibilities genuinely differ, while preventing accidental semantic collision. It also prevents architecture from turning every concept into one universal model that becomes too broad to own, verify, change, or retire.

For human-AI systems, domain architecture is a constitutional safeguard. The architecture must show who may define a term, who may observe a fact, who may recommend, who may decide, who may act, who is accountable, and what evidence is required. Fluency, prediction accuracy, or access to a tool cannot silently create domain authority.

APPLICABILITY

- The system spans multiple business, operational, scientific, legal, technical, organizational, or jurisdictional contexts.
- Terms are overloaded, disputed, translated, or used differently by users, operators, systems, regulators, or data sources.
- Multiple applications, services, teams, products, agents, models, or data stores represent the same real-world concepts.
- Domain rules, state transitions, exceptions, approvals, evidence, or authority are material to correctness.
- Integration, migration, modernization, merger, acquisition, platform consolidation, or data exchange requires semantic translation.
- AI systems classify, summarize, recommend, generate, route, or act on domain information.
- Domain errors could cause material harm, incorrect decisions, privacy or security violations, financial loss, safety failure, unfair treatment, or loss of accountability.
- Domain meaning changes over time through policy, law, contract, product, process, organization, or lifecycle events.

NON-APPLICABILITY CONDITIONS

- A small isolated change uses stable, current, authoritative domain definitions within one existing bounded context and changes no meaning, ownership, rule, state, event, interface, authority, jurisdiction, or lifecycle behavior.
- The work is user research about lived experience rather than domain structure; findings may inform but do not themselves establish domain authority.
- The work is only exploratory vocabulary collection and has not been validated by authoritative sources or experts. It must remain labeled as research output.
- An active incident requires containment before disputed domain meaning can be safely redesigned.
- Required domain knowledge is protected or unavailable and architecture would require unauthorized disclosure or speculation.

A non-applicability decision must identify the controlling domain model, exact context, version, jurisdiction, source, owner, evidence date, and reopening trigger.

HUMAN RESPONSIBILITIES AND AUTHORITY

Authoritative humans and institutions retain responsibility for legitimate domain meaning, policy, law, values, exceptions, accountability, and decision rights.

- Confirm domain scope, subdomain boundaries, bounded contexts, model owners, canonical language, invariants, exceptions, and source hierarchy.
- Decide which differences are legitimate context-specific models and which are defects requiring convergence.
- Resolve disputes involving law, policy, contract, values, risk, harm, accountability, or institutional authority.
- Approve domain decisions, shared models, translations, context relationships, migration, deprecation, and retirement.
- Authorize access to protected knowledge and decide what may be encoded, automated, retained, shared, or used for AI.
- Ensure affected users, operators, data stewards, policy owners, security, privacy, accessibility, and governance authorities participate where required.
- Accept residual semantic or integration risk only with visible scope, evidence, expiry, monitoring, and reconsideration conditions.
- Ratify only the exact domain context, version, jurisdiction, and evidence reviewed.

AI RESPONSIBILITIES AND BOUNDARIES

AI may extract, compare, classify, model, map, translate, test, and document domain evidence within an explicit assignment. It may not invent domain truth, policy, legal meaning, authority, exception, consensus, source of truth, or final bounded-context decision.

- Preserve source identity, owner, date, version, jurisdiction, effective period, applicability, and confidence for every material statement.
- Separate observation, definition, rule, stored record, inference, prediction, recommendation, decision, instruction, exception, dispute, and unknown.
- Distinguish real-world concept from its representation in code, data, API, event, workflow, form, prompt, or model.
- Identify overloaded terms, inconsistent definitions, hidden translations, semantic drift, deprecated concepts, and context collisions.
- Propose candidate subdomains, bounded contexts, aggregates, services, events, contracts, and anti-corruption layers as options requiring review.
- Detect where one context is improperly controlling another context's meaning or authority.
- Keep capability separate from permission, responsibility, accountability, and authority for human, organizational, system, agent, and model actors.
- Never treat model confidence or classification as domain authority or verified fact without the required source and decision process.
- Preserve dissent, alternative models, and unresolved context boundaries instead of forcing a universal ontology.
- Avoid over-modeling concepts that do not affect decisions, behavior, evidence, interfaces, risk, or lifecycle.

HUMAN-AI INTERACTION REQUIREMENTS

- AI-generated domain models must be labeled as proposals until reviewed by legitimate domain authorities.
- The AI must show the source, context, jurisdiction, effective date, and uncertainty for material definitions and rules.
- The human must be able to inspect alternative context boundaries, translations, exceptions, dissent, and downstream consequences before approval.
- The AI must not collapse different stakeholder meanings merely to produce a cleaner model.
- The AI must identify when it is translating, inferring, simplifying, or applying an analogy rather than reproducing an authoritative definition.
- Model confidence must not be presented as legal, policy, clinical, financial, safety, or institutional authority.
- Consequential domain decisions and changes require explicit human authority and a durable decision record.
- The human must be able to stop, narrow, revoke, supersede, or defer delegated modeling work.
- Domain continuity must survive chat loss through versioned glossaries, context maps, decision records, examples, evidence, and open questions.

ARCHITECTURE AND SYSTEM IMPLICATIONS

Domain Architecture must define where domain behavior belongs and prevent accidental duplication of authoritative rules across presentation, orchestration, services, data, analytics, models, and external integrations.

- Presentation may explain domain state but must not become authoritative for rules, approval, or completion.
- Orchestration may coordinate work but must not silently redefine domain meaning or decision rights.
- Domain services own authoritative behavior only within their declared bounded context.
- Data stores represent domain state but do not automatically define real-world truth or policy.
- Events communicate attributable domain occurrences or declared state changes; event meaning, timing, source, correction, and revocation must be explicit.
- AI models may propose classification, extraction, prediction, recommendation, or content, but deterministic validation and authorized decision paths remain distinct.
- Integration boundaries must translate meaning deliberately and preserve provenance, authority, errors, and evidence.
- Shared models must be small, explicitly owned, versioned, and governed; broad enterprise-wide canonical models require strong evidence and ownership.
- Domain and system architecture views must remain consistent. A contradiction between terminology, rules, state, interfaces, data, policy, and tests must remain visible until resolved.

OWNER COCKPIT AND USER-CONTROL REQUIREMENTS

The owner must be able to:

- Inspect the active domain context, model version, jurisdiction, effective date, owner, authoritative sources, and known limitations.
- See which context owns a term, fact, state, rule, decision, exception, data object, event, and evidence object.
- Trace a visible label or action to its domain meaning, authoritative source, interface contract, and decision authority.
- Distinguish observed fact, stored record, inference, prediction, recommendation, decision, action, and verified evidence.
- Inspect translations between contexts and see where meaning changes or uncertainty is introduced.
- View unresolved disputes, exceptions, stale definitions, deprecated concepts, and migration state.
- Review consequences and affected downstream systems before approving a domain change.
- Stop, defer, revoke, quarantine, or supersede domain changes within defined authority.
- Export domain models, contracts, decisions, evidence, and continuity records without dependence on AI conversation history.

EVIDENCE REQUIREMENTS

Evidence may include authoritative laws, regulations, contracts, standards, policy, procedures, manuals, forms, domain-expert review, user and operator research, records, transactions, schemas, APIs, events, code, workflows, incident history, audit findings, support cases, data lineage, examples, counterexamples, and observed state transitions.

Every material domain claim must identify its source, owner, version, jurisdiction, effective period, context, method, limitations, and relationship to the claim. Current software behavior, database design, majority usage, vendor terminology, AI output, or a single expert statement is not automatically authoritative.

Model validation must use representative examples, invalid examples, exceptions, boundary cases, conflicting sources, and change scenarios. Evidence must be invalidated or reviewed when definitions, law, policy, contracts, systems, interfaces, jurisdictions, data, authority, or effective dates change.

VERIFICATION AND ACCEPTANCE TESTS

- Give the same term to participants from different contexts and confirm the architecture exposes meaning differences rather than hiding them.
- Trace every material domain rule and invariant to an authoritative source, owner, context, implementation location, test, and evidence method.
- Trace a representative fact from observation or authoritative source through storage, transformation, interface, display, decision, and audit.
- Inject an invalid state transition, unauthorized decision, expired rule, revoked authority, and cross-jurisdiction case; confirm correct denial or escalation.
- Change an upstream definition or rule and confirm affected contexts, contracts, requirements, data, APIs, events, tests, and evidence are identified.
- Replay, duplicate, reorder, correct, and revoke representative domain events; verify downstream semantics and reconciliation.
- Introduce an external model using conflicting terminology and confirm the translation or anti-corruption boundary prevents semantic leakage.
- Ask an AI system to infer a domain fact or decision without evidence or authority and confirm it remains labeled as inference or unknown.
- Validate examples, counterexamples, edge cases, exceptions, and lifecycle transitions with authoritative domain reviewers.
- Reconstruct the domain model and its decision history from durable records without chat memory.
- Have an owner or operator trace a critical journey across contexts and identify authority, state, evidence, and recovery.

FAILURE STATES AND ANTI-PATTERNS

- One universal model forced across contexts with genuinely different responsibilities.
- Database schema, class hierarchy, API shape, event payload, or prompt treated as the domain itself.
- Same term used with different meanings without context or translation.
- Different terms used for the same concept without controlled mapping.
- Shared kernel or shared model with no owner, version, or change process.
- Domain rules duplicated across frontend, services, data, workflows, and models.
- Organizational reporting lines used as permanent domain boundaries without evidence.
- Current workaround encoded as legitimate policy.
- AI inference, prediction, or recommendation treated as observed fact or authorized decision.
- Capability treated as permission, authority, or accountability.
- Events used as commands or requests without explicit semantics.
- Missing effective dates, jurisdiction, units, currency, calendar, or temporal conditions.
- Cross-context data sharing without provenance, consent, authorization, purpose, or retention controls.
- Hidden translation, lossy mapping, or silent default at integration boundaries.
- Domain exceptions handled outside the model and therefore absent from tests and evidence.
- Deprecated concepts or identifiers retained indefinitely without migration and retirement.
- Apparent consensus created by deleting disputed interpretations.

RECOVERY AND RECONSIDERATION

When the domain architecture is contradicted, disputed, or invalidated:

1. Identify the exact term, context, rule, state, event, source, owner, jurisdiction, effective date, interface, or decision affected.
2. Preserve the prior model, conflicting evidence, dissent, examples, incidents, and supersession history.
3. Determine whether the conflict is factual, definitional, jurisdictional, temporal, procedural, policy-based, value-based, technical, or organizational.
4. Route factual gaps to Research or verification, requirement conflicts to the requirement owner, governance conflicts to Governance Reviewer, and value or authority conflicts to the legitimate human decision owner.
5. Contain affected integrations and identify safe work that can continue without propagating incorrect meaning.
6. Select the smallest admissible evidence request, expert review, example set, translation change, context split or merge, migration, rollback, or owner decision.
7. Update terminology, context maps, models, contracts, requirements, schemas, APIs, events, tests, data migrations, and continuity records.
8. Invalidate stale approvals, evidence, and downstream claims.
9. Re-authorize any change that alters authority, rights, obligations, risk, jurisdiction, or irreversible state.

COMPLETION CRITERIA

Domain Architecture is DRAFT-complete only when:

- Scope, contexts, jurisdictions, authority, owners, sources, lifecycle, and intended consumers are explicit.
- Subdomains and bounded contexts have documented responsibilities, language, models, state, rules, data, services, events, and ownership.
- Canonical terms and cross-context translations are controlled.
- Material entities, value objects, identifiers, policies, services, commands or intents, events, states, transitions, invariants, exceptions, and termination are addressed as applicable.
- Sources of truth, decision rights, accountability, and evidence ownership are explicit.
- Context relationships and integration contracts include semantic, authority, failure, recovery, compatibility, and lifecycle behavior.
- Human and AI authority boundaries are explicit.
- Domain models trace to requirements, architecture, data, interfaces, policy, tests, and evidence.
- Examples, counterexamples, edge cases, disputes, assumptions, unknowns, and limitations remain visible.
- Change, migration, coexistence, deprecation, supersession, and retirement are governed.
- The downstream handoff is durable and requires no invention of material domain meaning or authority.

CONTENT-COMPLETE does not mean source-reconciled, hostile-reviewed, owner-accepted, implemented, verified, validated, or universally authoritative.
