SECTION 021 — Enterprise Architecture

STATUS: CONTENT-COMPLETE

PURPOSE

Translate the authorized mission, business strategy, product and portfolio direction, requirements baseline, system definition, operating realities, and lifecycle obligations into a coherent enterprise-wide structure for capabilities, value streams, organizations, information, applications, technology, governance, investment, change, and retirement.

Enterprise Architecture governs alignment across an enterprise or similarly broad socio-technical boundary. It does not merely catalogue technology, publish diagrams, or impose uniformity. It determines which capabilities the enterprise must sustain, where decision authority and accountability reside, how business and technical domains relate, which standards and shared services are justified, what variation is legitimate, how current and target states differ, how transitions will be governed, and how architecture claims will be proven.

REQUIRED OUTCOME

- A declared enterprise boundary identifying mission, owners, operating environments, business units, products, programs, platforms, partners, suppliers, regulators, users, operators, affected parties, external systems, and lifecycle horizon.
- An enterprise operating model showing how value is created, decisions are made, responsibilities are distributed, capabilities are owned, information flows, shared services are governed, and exceptions are handled.
- A capability map with stable names, definitions, owners, outcomes, maturity or condition, dependencies, criticality, evidence, and lifecycle state.
- Value-stream and journey views connecting mission and stakeholder outcomes to capabilities, processes, information, applications, technology, people, controls, evidence, and operational results.
- Current-state, target-state, and transition-state architectures that distinguish observed fact, verified condition, inference, assumption, recommendation, approved decision, and unresolved unknown.
- Explicit architecture principles, constraints, standards, reference patterns, decision rights, exception paths, and review triggers.
- Portfolio-level alignment among strategies, investments, programs, products, platforms, data, security, governance, operations, maintenance, modernization, migration, deprecation, and retirement.
- A traceable architecture roadmap with transition increments, dependencies, prerequisites, evidence gates, owner decisions, rollback or recovery conditions, and retirement obligations.
- A durable architecture repository containing decisions, models, standards, waivers, exceptions, evidence, dissent, risks, dependencies, and supersession history.
- A bounded human-AI operating model in which AI can analyze and propose enterprise structures without converting capability into authority, preferences into policy, or generated models into verified enterprise truth.

WHY THIS MATTERS

Enterprises often accumulate products, systems, data stores, vendors, processes, policies, teams, and operating practices faster than they accumulate shared understanding. Local decisions can be individually reasonable while collectively creating duplicate capabilities, contradictory rules, fragmented identity, incompatible data, hidden operational burden, unowned risk, brittle dependencies, and costly lock-in.

Enterprise Architecture provides a governed way to see and shape that whole. Its value is not the diagram itself. Its value is the ability to connect strategy to capabilities, capabilities to investments and systems, systems to evidence and operation, and change to accountable human decisions.

AI can help discover duplication, map dependencies, compare transition options, and maintain traceability. It can also confidently infer an enterprise that does not exist, erase legitimate local variation, recommend centralization without understanding human consequences, or present a generated target state as an approved plan. This section requires AI-supported architecture to remain source-bound, challengeable, reversible where practical, and subordinate to legitimate human authority.

APPLICABILITY

- Multiple products, programs, business units, platforms, applications, data domains, suppliers, or operating environments must support one mission or portfolio.
- Strategic objectives require coordinated changes across people, process, information, technology, governance, finance, security, operations, or lifecycle management.
- Shared capabilities, platforms, standards, services, data, identity, evidence, or infrastructure are being created, consolidated, separated, migrated, or retired.
- Existing architecture is fragmented, undocumented, inconsistent, duplicative, vendor-dependent, difficult to operate, or disconnected from strategy and owner outcomes.
- Investments or modernization decisions require comparison across current, target, and transition states.
- Regulatory, legal, privacy, safety, security, accessibility, resilience, or records obligations apply across more than one system or organizational boundary.
- AI systems, agents, models, or automation influence enterprise decisions, shared data, common services, workforce processes, customer outcomes, or protected state.
- A merger, acquisition, divestiture, partnership, outsourcing arrangement, major reorganization, or platform transition changes enterprise boundaries or dependencies.

NON-APPLICABILITY CONDITIONS

- The work is confined to one bounded solution whose enterprise dependencies, standards, shared capabilities, data, authorities, and lifecycle obligations are already current, explicit, and verified.
- A current approved enterprise baseline covers the exact scope and proposed change, and impact analysis demonstrates no material effect on shared capabilities, standards, data, identity, security, operations, investment, or retirement.
- The organization has not authorized enterprise-level change; exploratory analysis may continue, but it must be labeled advisory and may not be represented as policy or approved target state.
- An active incident, legal prohibition, missing authority, or unsafe condition requires containment before normal architecture planning continues.
- Required enterprise information cannot be accessed lawfully or safely.

A non-applicability decision must identify the controlling baseline, scope, version, environment, authority, evidence, and reopening trigger. Small organizational size, informal culture, or use of AI-generated diagrams is not sufficient reason to omit enterprise-level alignment when the consequences are enterprise-wide.

PREREQUISITES AND DEPENDENCIES

- Sections 001–020 as applicable, especially the authenticated objective, strategies, portfolio and program boundaries, affected parties, domain model, feasibility conditions, risk treatments, approved requirements baseline, system of interest, operational concept, interfaces, lifecycle model, and evidence obligations.
- Legitimate human authority to define enterprise scope, principles, standards, investment priorities, organizational responsibilities, shared services, exceptions, risk acceptance, and target-state commitments.
- Current-state evidence for capabilities, processes, applications, data, integrations, infrastructure, suppliers, contracts, policies, incidents, costs, operational performance, maintenance burden, and retirement obligations.
- A controlled vocabulary for enterprise, capability, value stream, product, platform, service, domain, application, data product, technology, standard, principle, exception, transition, and retirement.
- A durable architecture repository or equivalent record supporting versioning, provenance, relationships, decisions, exceptions, evidence, and supersession.
- Defined architecture governance, review, escalation, exception, and change-control mechanisms.

REQUIRED INPUTS

- Mission and business strategies, product and portfolio strategies, operating model, financial constraints, investment horizon, and explicit non-goals.
- Approved requirements and systems baselines relevant to the enterprise scope.
- Capability inventories, capability owners, performance evidence, criticality, maturity, dependencies, and known gaps.
- Value streams, owner and user journeys, business processes, organizational responsibilities, service relationships, and operating procedures.
- Information and data domains, sources of truth, classifications, quality evidence, lineage, retention, privacy, and sovereignty requirements.
- Application, platform, integration, API, event, infrastructure, cloud, edge, network, identity, security, observability, and operations inventories.
- Supplier, partner, vendor, licensing, procurement, service-level, support, and exit obligations.
- Risks, incidents, control findings, technical debt, resilience limits, accessibility gaps, and maintenance evidence.
- Existing architecture principles, standards, reference patterns, waivers, exceptions, roadmaps, and decision records.
- AI-system identities, model and provider dependencies, data boundaries, evaluation evidence, fallback paths, revocation mechanisms, and human-oversight obligations.

HUMAN RESPONSIBILITIES AND AUTHORITY

Authorized humans retain authority for mission, enterprise boundary, values, operating model, investment, organizational design, policy, standards, risk acceptance, exceptions, commitments, and target-state approval.

- Confirm the enterprise scope, owners, decision rights, affected parties, and intended architecture horizon.
- Decide which capabilities are strategic, differentiating, shared, commodity, outsourced, prohibited, transitional, or scheduled for retirement.
- Approve architecture principles, mandatory standards, exception criteria, transition priorities, and resource commitments.
- Resolve conflicts involving values, rights, workforce impact, customer impact, privacy, accessibility, safety, security, resilience, cost, schedule, and strategic tradeoffs.
- Ensure that architecture governance does not become unaccountable bureaucracy or a mechanism for hiding owner decisions behind committees, frameworks, or model output.
- Ratify enterprise target states and material transition decisions over exact versions and evidence.

AI RESPONSIBILITIES AND BOUNDARIES

AI may inventory, classify, compare, trace, model, simulate, summarize, detect inconsistency, propose alternatives, maintain records, and assist impact analysis within an explicit assignment.

AI must:

- Preserve the authorized objective and scope.
- Distinguish observed current state from inferred or proposed architecture.
- Identify source identity, currency, coverage, conflicts, and unknowns.
- Keep capability, organization, application, data, platform, and technology concepts distinct.
- Present alternatives, tradeoffs, dependencies, transition costs, and reversal conditions.
- Surface dissent, local constraints, and affected-party consequences rather than optimizing only for structural neatness.
- Avoid declaring an enterprise standard, target state, exception, investment, or retirement decision without human authority.
- Avoid treating tool access, model capability, or architecture-role assignment as permission to change enterprise state.
- Report when the evidence does not support a reliable enterprise view.

APPLICABLE EXISTING AGENT ROLES

The existing source-defined roles apply without modification:

- Architect for enterprise structure, boundaries, components, interfaces, flows, alternatives, and verification paths.
- Researcher for current-state, market, supplier, regulatory, technical, and organizational evidence.
- Analyst for capability, portfolio, cost, dependency, scenario, and transition analysis.
- Critic for contradictions, unsupported assumptions, hidden dependencies, and architecture theater.
- Security Auditor for enterprise trust, identity, access, supply-chain, evidence, and control boundaries.
- Planner for transition roadmaps, dependencies, gates, milestones, and contingencies.
- Coordinator for multi-domain workstreams, handoffs, conflicts, and provenance.
- Observer for neutral capture of current state, operating evidence, and changes.
- Verifier for architecture claims, baseline identity, traceability, and evidence sufficiency.
- Governance Reviewer for authority, policy, exception, and conformance boundaries.
- User Experience Reviewer for owner comprehension, accessibility, control, and enterprise-operability impacts.
- Decision Arbiter Advisory for structured comparison where criteria and authority are explicit.
- Documentation Writer and Memory Curator for durable architecture records and continuity.
- Red Team for adversarial challenge of centralization, bypass, dependency, migration, recovery, and governance assumptions.

REQUIRED ACTIVITIES

1. Establish the enterprise architecture charter: objective, scope, authority, decision owners, time horizon, exclusions, evidence expectations, and completion boundary.
2. Define the enterprise context and boundary, including external organizations, partners, regulators, users, communities, ecosystems, and dependencies.
3. Establish the current-state evidence baseline and identify confidence, freshness, ownership, and gaps.
4. Define or reconcile the enterprise operating model, decision rights, accountability, and service relationships.
5. Build the capability map and connect each capability to outcomes, owners, value streams, processes, information, applications, technology, controls, evidence, and lifecycle state.
6. Map value streams and critical journeys from initiating need through operational outcome, evidence, support, recovery, and closure.
7. Model current-state architecture across business, capability, process, information, data, application, integration, technology, security, governance, and operations views as applicable.
8. Identify duplication, fragmentation, gaps, concentration risk, unowned capability, hidden coupling, stale standards, unsupported technology, and retirement debt.
9. Define architecture principles and classify each as mandatory constraint, approved preference, recommendation, or experiment.
10. Develop credible target-state alternatives rather than one presumed answer.
11. Evaluate alternatives against mission alignment, requirements, feasibility, risk, human impact, cost, complexity, resilience, security, privacy, accessibility, maintainability, portability, supportability, and reversibility.
12. Define transition architectures that preserve continuity while moving between current and target states.
13. Produce a roadmap with increments, dependencies, decisions, evidence gates, migration and rollback conditions, training, support, and retirement work.
14. Define standards, reference architectures, shared services, and exception processes only where their enterprise value and enforcement authority are explicit.
15. Map investments, initiatives, products, platforms, and projects to capabilities and target-state outcomes.
16. Define architecture governance, review, exception, waiver, expiry, supersession, and reopening processes.
17. Establish architecture measures and anti-metrics that detect both progress and harmful optimization.
18. Hostile-review the architecture for bypass, false centralization, shadow systems, authority drift, vendor lock-in, stale data, migration failure, inaccessible control, and unowned recovery.
19. Verify that target-state claims are supported and that generated architecture is not represented as implemented or operational.
20. Preserve a durable handoff to Solution Architecture and the specialist architecture sections.

HUMAN-AI INTERACTION REQUIREMENTS

- The owner must be able to ask what the enterprise architecture is trying to accomplish, what evidence supports it, what is proposed, what is approved, what remains unknown, and what decision is required.
- AI-generated diagrams, capability maps, heat maps, scores, and roadmaps must disclose source coverage and uncertainty.
- Recommendations must remain distinct from enterprise decisions and policy.
- Material architecture alternatives and dissent must remain inspectable in owner language.
- Consequential changes require explicit authorization over exact scope, target, assumptions, and evidence.
- Delegation must be revocable, bounded by role and data access, and recorded in continuity artifacts.
- The owner must retain visible stop, defer, narrow, reject, and reopen paths.
- Architecture explanations must support both strategic comprehension and technical drill-down without hiding uncertainty behind jargon.

ARCHITECTURE AND SYSTEM IMPLICATIONS

Enterprise Architecture must connect, but not collapse, the following layers:

- Mission, strategy, policy, and operating model.
- Capabilities and value streams.
- Organizations, roles, authority, and accountability.
- Processes, services, products, and journeys.
- Information, data, knowledge, records, and evidence.
- Applications, platforms, integrations, APIs, and events.
- Technology, infrastructure, cloud, edge, networks, devices, and facilities.
- Security, identity, privacy, governance, compliance, safety, reliability, resilience, accessibility, and content.
- Delivery, operations, support, maintenance, migration, deprecation, and retirement.

Each layer must identify ownership, interfaces, dependencies, sources of truth, criticality, failure behavior, recovery, evidence, and lifecycle state. Shared architecture must not erase legitimate domain autonomy; local autonomy must not bypass enterprise constraints or create unowned enterprise risk.

OWNER COCKPIT AND USER-CONTROL REQUIREMENTS

The owner-facing architecture view must allow an authorized person to:

- See the current, target, and transition states and their evidence dates.
- Trace strategy to capabilities, initiatives, systems, dependencies, risks, and outcomes.
- Identify architecture decisions awaiting authority, approved exceptions, expiring waivers, and unresolved dissent.
- Understand which systems and capabilities are critical, degraded, duplicated, unsupported, or scheduled for retirement.
- Inspect the consequence, cost, reversibility, and recovery path of a proposed transition.
- Distinguish diagrams and plans from implemented, verified, and operational state.
- Stop or defer a transition without losing the current continuity record.
- Export the architecture record in durable, non-chat-dependent form.

EVIDENCE REQUIREMENTS

Evidence must be bound to source, version, environment, owner, date, method, scope, integrity, and claim. Relevant evidence may include:

- Approved strategies, requirements, systems baselines, policies, contracts, and decision records.
- Inventories, configuration records, repositories, schemas, runtime observations, telemetry, incidents, support records, and financial evidence.
- Capability-owner interviews, user and operator evidence, process observation, supplier evidence, and regulatory sources.
- Architecture repositories, dependency graphs, data lineage, application portfolios, interface inventories, and control evidence.
- Migration tests, pilot results, resilience tests, accessibility evaluations, security findings, and operational exercises.

A diagram, maturity score, vendor presentation, workshop consensus, or AI-generated synthesis is not sufficient evidence of current state, implementation, conformance, or readiness.

VERIFICATION AND ACCEPTANCE TESTS

- Trace a representative strategic objective through capability, value stream, system, initiative, evidence, and operational outcome.
- Select a capability and verify owner, definition, dependencies, supporting systems, evidence, risks, and lifecycle state.
- Enumerate consequential command or decision paths and confirm that authority and evidence boundaries remain consistent.
- Change a material source or dependency and confirm that affected architecture claims and approvals are invalidated or reopened.
- Test whether a local client, team, or AI agent can bypass enterprise policy or falsely claim an exception.
- Simulate loss of a major vendor, platform, identity service, data source, or operating site and inspect transition and recovery plans.
- Compare current-state evidence to architecture models and identify unsupported or stale entries.
- Have the owner explain the target state, material tradeoffs, unresolved decisions, and rollback conditions without relying on the architect's live interpretation.
- Verify that planned, implemented, verified, operational, and retired states remain distinct.

FAILURE STATES AND ANTI-PATTERNS

- Architecture theater: polished models without decision use, evidence, ownership, or operational consequence.
- Inventory masquerading as architecture.
- Target-state fiction unsupported by current-state evidence or transition capacity.
- Framework capture: forcing the enterprise into a template instead of preserving the actual objective and context.
- Centralization by default, or decentralization by default, without consequence analysis.
- Capability maps with no owners, outcomes, evidence, or lifecycle state.
- Standards with no authority, enforcement, exception process, or retirement path.
- Roadmaps that omit dependencies, migration, rollback, support, and human adoption.
- Technology-led strategy substitution.
- Vendor lock-in hidden as simplification.
- AI-generated certainty, invented inventories, or inferred authority.
- Architecture governance that blocks safe work while failing to control consequential work.
- Silent removal of dissent, local constraints, accessibility needs, workforce impacts, or affected-party concerns.
- Claiming enterprise conformance or readiness from workshop completion or diagram approval.

RECOVERY AND RECONSIDERATION

When enterprise architecture is incomplete, contradicted, stale, or rejected:

1. Identify the exact affected claim, model, standard, roadmap item, or decision.
2. Preserve the prior state, evidence, dissent, and decision history.
3. Classify the issue as source, scope, authority, data, modeling, feasibility, risk, governance, transition, implementation, or operational failure.
4. Determine what safe analysis or local work may continue.
5. Request the smallest evidence, experiment, owner decision, or scope reduction that can resolve the blocker.
6. Reopen affected capability, dependency, standard, investment, transition, and retirement decisions.
7. Reissue the architecture baseline with explicit supersession and downstream impact.

COMPLETION CRITERIA

Enterprise Architecture is DRAFT-complete only when:

- Scope, owner, authority, affected parties, and horizon are explicit.
- Current-state claims are source-bound and uncertainty is visible.
- Capabilities, value streams, responsibilities, information, applications, technology, and lifecycle obligations are connected.
- Target-state alternatives and tradeoffs are explicit.
- Transition architecture, dependencies, evidence gates, rollback, and retirement are defined.
- Principles, standards, exceptions, and governance have legitimate authority and review paths.
- Human and AI responsibilities remain bounded.
- Material decisions and dissent remain visible.
- Verification methods exist for material architecture claims.
- The owner can understand, challenge, approve, reject, or reopen the exact architecture state.

CONTENT-COMPLETE does not mean source-reconciled, hostile-reviewed, owner-accepted, implemented, verified, or operational.

HANDOFF AND CONTINUITY REQUIREMENTS

The handoff must include exact versions of the enterprise scope, current-state baseline, capability map, value streams, target alternatives, approved decisions, transition roadmap, principles, standards, exceptions, evidence, risks, dissent, open decisions, and next safe action.

Any downstream discovery that invalidates an enterprise assumption must reopen this section through a durable change record.
